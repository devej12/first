## 1. The structure of parliament

### House of Commons

- The UK Parliament is **bicameral**: House of Commons + House of Lords. Unlike almost every other legislature, one chamber (the Commons) holds nearly all real power.
- By convention, PMs and most ministers sit in the Commons. The last peer to become PM was **Alec Douglas-Home (1963)**, who resigned his peerage and won a by-election to sit as an MP.
- **Exception**: David (now Lord) Cameron was appointed foreign secretary in **2023** as an unelected peer — critics said he lacked democratic legitimacy and accountability.
- **650 MPs**, each directly elected in a single-member constituency under **first-past-the-post**.
- Plans to cut the number of MPs to **600** (drawn up in 2010 after the MPs' expenses scandal) have never been implemented.
- Average constituency electorate ≈ **73,000**. Largest in 2023: **North Shropshire (77,052)**. Smallest: **Na h-Eileanan an Iar / Western Isles (~21,000)**.
- The Commons is dominated by party politics, though 2024 saw a record **16 MPs** elected as independents or smaller-party members (5 Reform UK, 4 Greens, 4 independents in seats with large Muslim populations opposed to Labour's stance on the Israel–Gaza war, plus expelled former Labour leader Jeremy Corbyn as an independent).
- George Galloway won three by-elections for smaller parties, most recently **Rochdale, January 2024** (Workers Party of Britain), but lost the seat in the 2024 general election.

## House of Lords

- • Almost entirely unelected; no democratic mandate, hence few powers — largely advisory, able only to ask the Commons to "think again".
- • Membership not fixed in law; **just over 830 eligible peers in February 2025**. Composition:
  - ◦ **Life peers** — appointed for their lifetime (possible since the **Life Peerages Act 1958**). Most peers today; usually nominated by party leaders.
  - ◦ **Hereditary peers** — **92** remained, chosen by other hereditary peers after the **1999 Blair reforms** (before 1999 there were ~700). Ironic: the only "elected" part of the Lords. Legislation to remove the remaining hereditary peers was progressing in **2024/25** (a Keir Starmer 2024 election pledge).
  - ◦ **Church of England bishops** — **26**, mostly by seniority; the bishops of Canterbury, Durham, London, Winchester and York get automatic seats. Known collectively as the **Lords spiritual**.
  - ◦ Many independents (**crossbenchers**); no party has had a Lords majority since 1999.
- • Before 2009 the Law Lords also sat in the Lords; that ended with the creation of the separate UK Supreme Court.
- • The UK is unique among Western democracies in having an unelected second chamber — a long-criticised "democratic deficit". Suggested reforms include compulsory retirement at 80, removing hereditary peers, and more effective scrutiny of life peerage nominations.

|  For reform | Against reform  |
| --- | --- |
|  Lords has no real legislative power; its amendments can be and often are ignored | Performs its "think again" role well without blocking the elected government  |

### Trends and developments since the nineteenth century

- **Democracy:** reform Acts extended the franchise gradually (from the Great Reform Act 1832), reaching **full female suffrage in 1928**.
- **Balance of power:** the **Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949** shifted real power to the Commons.
- **Diversity:** first female MP to sit, **Nancy Astor, 1919**; record **263 women MPs** elected July 2024; first Chinese MP and first South Asian female MP both in **2010**; record **90 MPs from minority ethnic groups** after 2024. More LGBTQ+ representatives and MPs with disabilities too.
- **Checks and balances:** party discipline grew, reducing independent voting — but backbench rebellions have revived (e.g. Theresa May could not get her Brexit deal through parliament because of opposition from her own MPs, 2016–19).
- **Committees:** much parliamentary business now happens in committee, making the adversarial theatre of PMQs untypical of everyday business.
- **Broadcasting:** televised proceedings since **1989**.
- **Devolution and EU membership:** many policies/laws made outside Westminster (Edinburgh, Brussels until 2020), especially in devolved/EU areas like trade and agriculture.
- Ancient traditions persist: no electronic voting (MPs walk through the Aye/Noe division lobbies to be counted manually), government and opposition sit two sword lengths (3.96 m) apart, and the speaker's daily procession is led by the gold mace. Newly elected speakers are ceremonially "dragged" to the chair (the custom reflects the historic danger of the role).

### Key positions in parliament

- **Prime minister** — leader of the largest party, normally commands a Commons majority (2010–15 and 2017–19 were exceptions); in practice the executive dominates the legislature, so a PM with a big majority can usually get the Commons to vote as they wish.
- **Speaker** — chairs the Commons, keeps order, enforces House rules (e.g. MPs may not call another MP a liar; Labour's Dawn Butler was suspended in 2021 for calling Boris Johnson a liar and refusing to withdraw it). Elected by MPs via ballots: **Lindsay Hoyle** was elected on the fourth ballot in November 2019 and re-

elected unanimously in December 2019 and July 2024. By tradition the speaker renounces party allegiance and the major parties do not contest their seat. Recent controversies: Michael Martin (2000–09) was effectively forced out over the expenses scandal; John Bercow faced criticism over alleged favouritism towards opposition MPs, perceived lack of cooperation over Brexit legislation, and bullying allegations (denied) from staff including former private secretary Kate Emms.

- **Leader of the House of Commons** — cabinet-level "business manager" of the government (Lucy Powell after 2024); announces next week's business on Thursdays; liaises with the chief whip. Also holds the ceremonial role of Lord President of the Privy Council — Penny Mordaunt carried the Jewelled Sword of Offering at King Charles III's coronation.
- **Whips** — enforce party discipline (term comes from fox hunting's "whipper-in"); organise and press MPs to vote the leadership line.
- **Suspension of the whip** — temporary expulsion from the party parliamentary group, usually for bringing the party into disrepute:
  - Conservative Matt Hancock lost the whip in 2022 for appearing on I'm a Celebrity without permission.
  - After the first backbench rebellion of his premiership (July 2024), Keir Starmer suspended seven left-wing Labour rebels including John McDonnell for 6 months for backing an SNP amendment to end the two-child benefit cap; 42 more abstained unsanctioned.
  - Julian Lewis (Conservative) had the whip removed in July 2020 after winning the Intelligence and Security Committee chair by securing opposition votes, beating the government's preferred candidate Chris Grayling.
  - Jeremy Corbyn lost the whip over his response to the antisemitism inquiry and support for Stop the War, was later expelled, yet won Islington North as an independent in 2024, defeating official Labour.
- **Frontbenchers** — government ministers and opposition shadow ministers (they sit on the front rows. **Backbenchers** — MPs who hold neither role; the more independently minded MPs sit here.

## 2. The main functions of parliament

- **Legislative** — where laws are introduced, debated and passed.
- **Representative** — represents people geographically (constituencies) and politically (parties).
- **Scrutiny** — checks and questions government actions and legislative plans.
- **Deliberative** — a national forum for debate; the focal point in crises (wars, Brexit, Covid-19: while Boris Johnson gave daily TV briefings, parliament passed the Coronavirus Act 2020).

## 3. Legislation and the legislative process

### Bill types

- **Public bills** apply to everyone once law (most legislation).
- **Private bills** apply only to specific institutions or bodies — narrow and uncontroversial (e.g. Bishop's Stortford Cemetery Act 2024).
- **Private members' bills (PMBs)** — introduced by individual MPs or peers.

Session 2023–24 (cut short by the May dissolution): **24 bills passed — 18 government, 5 PMBs, 1 private.**
Around **30–40 public bills** pass each year on average (57 in 2023).

### Stages of a bill

1. **First reading** — formal introduction, no debate.

--- Page 5 ---

2. **Second reading** — the main debate on the bill's principles.
3. **Committee stage** — a public bill committee goes through the bill line by line and can amend it.
4. **Report stage** — the whole Commons considers committee amendments, accepting, rejecting or changing them; further amendments possible.
5. **Third reading** — final debate on the amended bill; no further changes.
6. **Lords stages** — process repeated in the Lords; its amendments only stand if the Commons accepts them, so bills can bounce back and forth ("parliamentary ping-pong").
7. **Royal assent** — a formality today.

## Secondary legislation

- **Statutory instruments (SIs)** — minister-made law under powers in primary legislation, mainly for efficiency. Around **3,500 a year**, far exceeding Acts of Parliament.
- Parliament must approve SIs but **cannot amend** them; scrutiny by the **Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments** (one of the few committees with no government majority).
- Overall, SIs receive much less scrutiny than primary legislation.

## The King's Speech (case study, 2024)

- Read by the monarch at the State Opening of Parliament, but **written by the PM**; not exhaustive (e.g. emergency Covid laws); contents rarely a surprise — a statement of priorities.
- **2024: forty bills, the most since 2005**; included carry-overs (football regulator for English football; phasing out tobacco sales) and Labour manifesto items (National Wealth Fund, planning overhaul for housebuilding, gradual rail renationalisation in England).
- Notable omissions: votes at 16; compulsory retirement (the hereditary peers issue was left to a separate bill).
- Synoptic link: unlike the King's Speech, the US State of the Union is a request to Congress, reflecting the separation of powers.

## Case study: the Rwanda asylum scheme

- **April 2022:** Boris Johnson announces plans to deport small-boat arrivals to Rwanda.
- **June 2022:** first flight cancelled after European Court of Human Rights injunctions.
- **October 2022:** Home Secretary Suella Braverman calls a Rwanda deportation flight her "dream" and "obsession" at Conservative conference.
- **March 2023:** Braverman introduces the Illegal Migration Bill (law by July 2023) — duty to detain and remove those arriving without permission.
- **November 2023:** Supreme Court unanimously rules the policy unlawful (Rwanda not properly assessed as safe).
- **December 2023:** new UK–Rwanda treaty (5 December); Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Bill introduced next day to override the ruling and block Strasbourg intervention.
- **March 2024:** National Audit Office says the scheme would cost **£1.8m per person for the first 300 deported**, and **~£370m over 5 years even if nobody is sent**.
- **April 2024:** the Act finally passes after repeated Lords attempts to amend/block.
- **July 2024:** Labour drops the scheme; Yvette Cooper puts the total cost at **£700m**.

## Private members' bills

- System dates from the late 1940s: some parliamentary time reserved for individual MPs' and peers' bills — a route for backbenchers to respond to public concerns, especially non-party issues. Key laws that began

--- Page 6 ---

as PMBs: **Abortion Act 1967**, abolition of capital punishment (1965), and (partially) the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill, which passed its initial stage in November 2024 with cabinet members voting both ways.

- **Thirteen Friday sittings (~65 hours)** per year; the first seven Fridays go to ballot bills.
- **Ballot bills** — 20 names drawn; best chance of becoming law or proper debate. Lucky MPs may adopt pressure-group proposals or government "handout" bills (technical changes the government lacks time for or prefers not to steer itself). Blocked if controversial: the "Turing Bill" (2016 pardons for pre-1967 same-sex convictions) failed after a minister spoke until time ran out; Conservative MP Christopher Chope's single objection blocked the "upskirting" bill in 2018 (a government-backed bill later passed). Only **four ballot bills** passed in the shortened 2023–24 session.
- **Ten minute rule bills** — 10-minute speaking slots after Question Time on Tuesdays and Wednesdays, allotted by the whips; mainly a way to raise issues. Rare success: **Guardianship (Missing Persons) Act 2017** (Kevin Hollinrake).
- **Presentation bills** — any MP may introduce one with notice to the Public Bill Office, presented on Fridays after ballot bills; no speech or debate, so useful only for discrete, non-controversial issues.
- **Success factors:** uncontroversial; lucky in the ballot; government support (or at least non-opposition); cross-party/public/media backing; time.
- 2024 PMB examples: Pet Abduction Act (abducting a cat, dog or other common domestic pet made a specific offence); Paternity Leave (Bereavement) Act.

## 4. Parliamentary representation

### How well does the Commons represent? (debate)

|  Performs well | Performs poorly  |
| --- | --- |
|  650 roughly equal constituencies (avg ~73,000); boundaries drawn independently by the Boundary Commission | Constituencies unequal: three times more voters in the largest than the smallest (mostly protected island seats like Orkney and Shetland)  |
|  Wide range of parties represented, from right-wing Reform UK to left-wing Greens | FPTP favours large/regionally concentrated parties (SNP) and under-represents Greens and Reform UK  |
|  Growing diversity: record 263 women in 2024; 10% of MPs openly identify as LGBTQ+ (vs ~3% of the population); record 14% from minority ethnic backgrounds | Women under-represented (only 40% of the 2024 intake, though up from 34% in 2019); minority ethnic MPs below the population average of 18%  |
|  MPs represent personal policy interests (disability, mental health, animal welfare, football) | MPs' backgrounds unrepresentative: 23% privately educated vs 7% of the population, even in the most diverse Commons ever  |

### MPs as part-time representatives

- Many MPs keep outside jobs (consultancy, journalism, TV). Jacob Rees-Mogg was paid **£29,000 a month** for a GB News show. Sky News (early 2023): about **20 MPs had earned over £160,000** in outside earnings since 2019, including Boris Johnson, Theresa May and Jess Phillips.
- Nothing illegal, but questions arise over "cashing in" and time for constituents. Counter-example: former nurse Maria Caulfield said she needed 480 hours over 3 years to keep her professional registration.

### MPs at work

- MPs informally represent causes via all-party groups, and act as constituency problem-solvers through surgeries and casework (housing, immigration and more), for all constituents regardless of how they voted. Casework loads can be enormous — The Guardian reported in 2021 that some MPs' casework had risen

## Theories of representation

- **Burkean / trustee theory** — from Edmund Burke (MP, 1774, Bristol): once elected, an MP owes constituents their judgement and conscience, not obedience; voters entrust the MP to decide. His famous formulation: the representative owes you "not his industry only, but his judgement", and betrays you "if he sacrifices it to your opinion". Weakened by party discipline, but visible in unwhipped conscience votes (abortion, assisted dying) and occasionally on party lines (e.g. Nick Boles, a Remainer representing a Leave seat). Theories overlap — the same MP may act differently depending on the issue.
- **Delegate theory** — MPs as pure mouthpieces for constituents' views. Hard in practice (how to know constituents' majority view on every issue?):
  - Zac Goldsmith promised to resign if government backed a third Heathrow runway; he did, resigning in 2016 and losing the by-election to the Liberal Democrats.
  - Stephen Lloyd (LD, Eastbourne, a Leave seat) pledged to back Brexit if re-elected in 2017, and kept it against his own views and party policy.
  - Neither was rewarded by voters, suggesting delegate loyalty does not pay.
- **Mandate theory** — MPs exist to carry out their party's manifesto; the party, not the person, won the seat. Evidence: MPs who defect or lose the whip and restand almost always lose (e.g. Faiza Shaheen, deselected by Labour over antisemitism allegations, stood as an independent in 2024 and lost). But new issues arise without manifesto cover: in December 2021, **99 Tory MPs rebelled** over new Covid-19 regulations, which passed only with opposition support.
- **Verdict**: all three operate, but mandate theory usually prevails; deviations reflect strong local feeling or post-manifesto controversies.

## Case study: Kim Leadbeater's assisted dying bill

- Commons vote, 30 November 2024, on the PMB to allow assisted dying in tightly controlled circumstances — genuinely uncertain outcome. A similar measure had been defeated 330–118 in 2015; a decade of new MPs and shifted public opinion (polls showing majority support, celebrity backing) changed the context. Intense lobbying from Dignity in Dying (for) and Care Not Killing and religious leaders (against); some MPs decided only during the debate.
- Passed **330–275**. Splits: Labour 234 for / 147 against; Conservatives 23 / 92; Liberal Democrats 61 / 12; Reform UK 3 / 2; Greens 4 / 0.
- A textbook example of MPs acting as Burkean trustees: unwhipped, following conscience, some holding special meetings with constituents.

## 5. Scrutiny of the executive

Main methods: **debates, parliamentary questions, committees**.

### Parliamentary debates

- **Syrian air strikes (2013)**: David Cameron proposed strikes after chemical weapon attacks by Assad. He was defeated in the Commons — a rare government defeat on military action — and accepted that parliament, reflecting the people, did not want action. Parliament effectively took the lead on foreign policy (military action is a prerogative power, but modern convention has PMs seek Commons approval).
- **December 2015**: Commons voted to support air strikes on Islamic State — shortly after IS-linked attacks in Paris killed 130.

--- Page 8 ---

- **Contaminated blood scandal:** non-partisan debate moved by Labour MP Diana Johnson in July 2017 (patients given infected blood, contracting hepatitis C and HIV); credited pressure groups such as the Contaminated Blood Campaign.
- **Emergency debates:** granted at the speaker's discretion for "specific and important" urgent matters; few are allowed (the SNP was refused one on Gaza, February 2024).

- **Recent developments:**

- **Backbench Business Committee** (2010, from the Wright Committee proposals, 2009) — decides topics for about a day a week of Commons and Westminster Hall debate.
- **Westminster Hall debates** — 4 days a week, allocated by ballot via the Speaker's Office; any MP may attend; no votes; often poorly attended but a chance to raise local and national issues. Mondays cover petitions; e-petitions passing 100,000 signatures must be considered for debate by the Petitions Committee (not guaranteed). Example: the 2017 petition against Trump's state visit (over 1m signatures) triggered debate but did not change policy.
- **Why debates often don't check government:** MPs' minds are rarely changed; the whip system and majority guarantee government wins; the **payroll vote** (ministers, junior ministers and PPSs bound to support the government) has grown from 101 in 1960 to **171 in January 2025**; budget votes are effectively confidence votes with only ~4 days of debate, which the Democratic Audit (2018) called general political "talk-fests"; Lords amendments have limited effect (e.g. of the Lords' five amendments to Johnson's 2020 Brexit bill, none was accepted).
- **Parliamentary privilege** (from the 1689 Bill of Rights): MPs and peers speaking in parliament are free from slander and contempt of court laws. Recent uses: Lord Hain (2018) named Sir Philip Green despite an injunction; David Davis (2024) asked why UK readers were blocked from a US magazine article on the Lucy Letby case.

## Parliamentary questions

- Written or oral; **over 73,000 questions in 2022–23**, mostly to big-spending departments (health, education).
- **PMQs:** every Wednesday, noon, 30 minutes. Government backbenchers ask flattering "patsy" questions; the exchanges are famous for theatre and insults — e.g. May on Corbyn over Trident ("He says one thing to the many, and another to the few", 2017); Starmer on Sunak ("beaten by a lettuce", referring to Liz Truss's 49 days, 2022).
- **Effectiveness debate (condensed):** for — high-profile, forces the PM to address key issues, the most direct daily scrutiny most heads of government never face; against — theatrical "Punch and Judy" combat, questions often planted, rarely changes policy, rewards soundbites.
- **Urgent questions (UQs):** rising in number; granted at the speaker's discretion that morning. 56 UQs in 2023–24 (160 in the previous session), covering Ukraine, Israel–Gaza, steel, net migration.

## Committees

- **Public bill committees** — scrutinise bills line by line in the committee stage. Effectiveness debated: a 2013 UCL Constitution Unit report found bill scrutiny in committees to be largely weak; membership is still controlled by party whips.
- **Select committees** — the most significant committees:
  - The **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** dates from 1857 and examines government efficiency/spending.
  - Since **1979** every government department has been "shadowed" by a departmental select committee.
  - Chairs elected by MPs (using the alternative vote), members by secret ballot since the Wright reforms — reducing whip control.
  - Powers: summon witnesses and papers, quasi-courtroom questioning of ministers and officials; government must respond to reports within 60 days; increasingly high media profile.

◦ Review some major public appointments since 2007: Charlotte Hogg resigned as Bank of England Deputy Governor in 2017 after Treasury Committee criticism of her answers.
◦ The **Liaison Committee** (all select committee chairs) questions the PM about three times a year.
◦ Civil servants give evidence as representatives of ministers (per the Civil Service Code and the Osmotherly Rules), not expressing personal policy views, but remain personally accountable for delivery.
◦ **Case study:** Health and Social Care Select Committee inquiry into assisted dying/assisted suicide — evidence from Dignity in Dying, Care Not Killing, the relevant minister and healthcare professionals, plus 68,000 public responses; recommendations included better hospice/palliative care access and clearer BMA/GMC guidance.
◦ **Effectiveness debate (condensed):** effective — less partisan, opposition chairs common, recommendations often adopted, expert membership, elected chairs, appointment scrutiny. Weak — government majority on every committee, recommendations often rejected, party loyalties persist (125 of 1,325 reports saw votes 2010–19, often on party lines), topics can avoid politicised "low-hanging fruit", no veto power (unlike US Senate confirmations — cf. Baroness Stowell), whips still control public bill committee membership.
◦ Common exam error: select committees do not scrutinise legislation — that is public bill committees' job (select committees only consider some draft bills).
• **Lords committees** — least significant; do not shadow departments but run specialist inquiries (e.g. International Agreements Committee, Constitution Committee, Science and Technology Committee), exploiting the Lords' expertise; usually 12 members with no government majority; plus ~4 short-term special inquiry committees a year.
• **Intelligence and Security Committee of Parliament** — statutory (Intelligence Services Act 1994); oversees MI5, MI6 and GCHQ; members of both houses, selected via whips but electing their own chair — which produced the 2020 upset when Julian Lewis beat Chris Grayling to the chair and lost the whip.

## The roles and influence of MPs and peers

• A backbencher's day spans Westminster Hall debates, media interviews, constituency casework, all-party groups, committee evidence sessions, urgent debates, meetings with whips, fundraising and party business (see the diary study adapted from the Committee on Standards in Public Life, 2018).
• Influence channels: voting (and occasionally rebelling), PMBs, debates, oral/written questions, informal lobbying of ministers, committee membership, all-party groups.

## 6. The opposition

• **Four forms:** the Official Opposition (Labour or Conservative since the 1920s); other opposition parties; intra-party opposition (within the governing party); inter-party opposition (within coalitions, e.g. 2010–15).
• **Purpose:** scrutinise and check government, and offer viable alternatives (spending promises must be costed).
• **Tools and privileges:** the opposition leader opens PMQs with up to six questions and extra salary (with three opposition whips); **Short money** supports policy research; **20 opposition days** a year (17 to the Official Opposition, 3 to the second-largest opposition party) to choose debate topics; select committee reports; coalition-era example: Lib Dem intra-party opposition killed Conservative inheritance tax cut proposals; 2018–19: opposition MPs plus Tory rebels blocked successive versions of May's Brexit deal.
• **Significance factors:** parliamentary arithmetic (small/no majority empowers opposition, e.g. May 2017–19); national context (Covid-19 required visible support rather than point-scoring).

• Overall: the opposition opposes and proposes; a healthy system needs an "equilibrium of legitimacy" — the opposition accepts the elected government's right to govern, and the government accepts the opposition's legitimacy.

**Strengths and weaknesses (condensed):** strengths — funding/privileges, government-in-waiting positioning, real blocking power at moments of minority government or party division (Brexit). Weaknesses — usually cannot defeat a majority government; limited agenda control; exposure value high but concrete power low.

## 7. Parliament and government: who dominates?

• The UK has **fusion of powers**: the executive is formed from and remains within the legislature (contrast US separation of powers).
• **Executive dominance (for):** whipping and party discipline mean government bills pass largely unamended; the government controls most parliamentary time; PMBs almost always need government support and time to pass; the payroll vote binds ministers and PPSs; budgets pass as confidence votes.
• **Against dominance:** growing backbench rebellions since the 1980s (Brexit, Iraq, Syria, tuition fees); select committees increasingly independent and high-profile; coalition/minority periods (2010–15, 2017–19) forced negotiation; defeats are real events, not just exceptions — May's Brexit deal failures, the 2013 Syria vote, the 2020 ISC chair election.
• **How effective is scrutiny in practice?**
  ◦ Advances: more rebellious MPs; stronger select committees; recall petitions can now remove MPs between elections; more diverse membership; Lords reform (hereditary peers largely removed); parliament remains the arena for national crises; the UK avoids the frequent US-style gridlock; "we get the government we elect", and it can usually govern.
  ◦ Weaknesses: archaic language, tradition and adversarialism; no electronic voting; debates and questions often party theatre; weak checks on a strong majority government (Brexit showed parliament unable to take control and resolve deadlock itself — resolution came only via a new election); committees with limited impact on legislation and appointments; a weak, unelected second chamber full of political retirees.
• **Conclusion:** the answer depends on what you want from a legislature — hard scrutiny (UK scores poorly) or effective government with occasional accountability (the Westminster model works well enough).

## 8. Chapter summary

• Parliament = Commons + Lords; the Commons dominates, chaired by the speaker, with discipline enforced by whips.
• Recent developments include partial Lords reform (hereditary peers being removed in the 2024/25 session).
• Bills must clear multiple hurdles in both chambers; backbenchers can shape or introduce legislation through debates and PMBs.
• Representation works geographically, politically and through diversity; three models: Burkean/trustee, delegate, mandate.
• Parliament scrutinises through debates, questions and committees, though party politics often blunts effectiveness.
• Committees (public bill and select) are central to legislative and policy scrutiny; select committees are the most effective scrutiny innovation.
• The opposition holds government to account via debates, PMQs and alternative policies; the Official Opposition is a "government-in-waiting".
